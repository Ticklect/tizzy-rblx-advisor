# Packaging: core fantasy, title, thumbnail, CTR

## Core fantasy first

Before the creative, ask: what emotional/role fantasy is the player buying? Can a young player explain it to a friend in one sentence? Is the primary verb clear?

Strong fantasy is not merely a theme. "Collect data" is abstract; "hack a business" immediately implies role/action/stakes. This is a public case-study pattern, not a universal title formula.

## Title principles

- simple vocabulary;
- direct verb/noun or clear challenge can work well;
- communicate role/action/fantasy;
- implied challenge can increase curiosity;
- avoid technical/abstract wording the target audience may not visualize;
- keyword research can validate whether a fantasy/verb appears repeatedly in successful experiences, but do not clone names.

Do not literalize the transcript's "third-grade reading level" as a rigid law for every audience. Use it as a readability heuristic for mass-market youth games.

## Thumbnail principles

A strong thumbnail communicates in a fraction of a second:
- who/what matters;
- action/conflict;
- emotion/stakes;
- fantasy;
- optionally the unique mechanic.

Title gives context to image and image gives emotion/context to title. They should complement, not redundantly say the same thing.

## Research

Study top-performing creatives by pattern:
- face/pose/emotion;
- subject count;
- background simplicity;
- contrast/readability;
- mechanic/fantasy cue;
- phone-size legibility.

Copy functionality/pattern, not exact assets/composition.

## Test volume

The public corpus strongly favors multiple substantially different creative concepts and repeated testing. Do not turn a historical "10/20/50 creatives" anecdote into a universal minimum; the principle is enough variation to learn.

## CTR guardrail

Higher CTR/PTR is not automatically a win. If the creative/title overpromises, first-play bounce can worsen. Evaluate packaging and first-minute delivery together.
